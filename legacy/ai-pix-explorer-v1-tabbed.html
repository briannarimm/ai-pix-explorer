The Big Picture

What you're looking at

When you type a question into an AI research tool, three machines handle it, nested inside one another like a set of Russian dolls. The smallest doll is the large language model (LLM) itself. Around it sits retrieval (RAG), which fetches documents for the model to read. Around that sits the agent loop, which lets the system take actions, look at the results, and check its own work. Each tab in this program opens one of these machines. This page shows how they connect.

The LLM: autocomplete that read a library

Your phone's keyboard suggests the next word as you type. An LLM does the same thing at enormous scale. During training it read a large share of the public internet plus books, articles and code, and it played one game over and over: hide the next word, guess it, check the answer, adjust. After trillions of rounds it got extremely good at the game. Everything an LLM does, from answering questions to writing code, is that one game repeated: predict one small chunk of text (a token), add it to the page, then predict the next one.

Physically, the model is a very large file of numbers called weights or parameters, often hundreds of billions of them. Running the model means doing arithmetic with those numbers on your text. The weights are frozen once training ends. The model does not learn from your conversation; it only reads it, the way a calculator doesn't get smarter from the sums you type in.

RAG: turning a closed-book exam into an open-book one

A plain LLM takes every test closed-book. It answers from whatever stuck during training, which can be outdated, incomplete or wrong, and it can't tell you where it learned anything. Retrieval-augmented generation hands it the book. Before the model answers, a search system finds the most relevant passages in a document collection and pastes them into the text the model reads. The model then writes its answer from those passages and points to them. The model itself never changes. Only its reading material does.

Agents: a model that can send out for things

An agent is an LLM placed inside a loop with tools. Picture a chef who isn't allowed to leave the kitchen. The chef can't go to the market, but can write an order on a slip and hand it through the window to a runner. The runner fetches the item and brings it back, and the chef decides what to do next.

In software terms: the model writes text in a special format that means "run this tool with these inputs." Ordinary code wrapped around the model, called the harness, spots that text, runs the tool, and pastes the result back into the conversation. Then it calls the model again with the longer conversation. This repeats until the model writes a final answer instead of another order. The Agents tab walks through every step.

Click any box in the diagram to open its toy, or press Trace a question to follow one research question through the whole system. The 3D Run tab shows the same journey token by token, and Limits shows where all of this breaks down.

Token Chopper

Why text has to be chopped up

Computers do arithmetic on numbers, not on letters. Before an LLM can read anything, your text has to become a list of numbers. The tool that does this is the tokenizer, and the pieces it produces are tokens.

One number per word sounds natural but breaks down fast. English alone has hundreds of thousands of words, before you add names, typos, citations and new terms coined every week. One number per letter has the opposite problem: every sentence becomes very long, and the model has to rebuild every word from scratch. Tokenizers take the middle path.

Like a box of building bricks

Imagine a box of building bricks where the most common shapes come pre-assembled. "the" is one brick. "court" is one brick. "Beneficiary" is less common, so it comes as two or three smaller bricks snapped together. A citation like "456.4-420" gets built from little number and punctuation bricks. Nothing is ever impossible to build, because single characters are in the box too. The box has a fixed number of brick types, around 100,000 to 200,000 in current models.

How the brick set is chosen

The brick set is learned from data with a procedure called byte-pair encoding. Start with single characters. Scan a huge amount of text and find the pair of neighboring pieces that appears most often, say "t" followed by "h". Glue them into a new brick, "th". Repeat: maybe "th" plus "e" becomes "the". Keep merging the most frequent pair until the box holds as many bricks as you want.

The result is purely statistical. Frequent strings become single tokens and rare strings stay in pieces, which is why tokens don't line up with syllables or legal terms of art. A leading space is usually part of the token, so " court" with a space and "court" without one get different IDs.

What the model actually receives

After chopping, every token is swapped for its catalog number. The model never sees the word "court"; it sees something like 4389. Everything downstream works on these numbers.

The desk: the context window

Everything the model considers at once has to fit in its context window, measured in tokens. Think of it as the surface of a desk. The system's instructions, your question, any pasted documents, the conversation so far and the answer being written all have to fit on that desk. Whatever doesn't fit is simply absent. The model can't go and look for it.

Desks have grown from a few thousand tokens to hundreds of thousands, but a long trial record can still overflow one. That is a major reason RAG exists: rather than piling the whole record on the desk, you fetch the handful of pages that matter.

Tokens also drive cost and speed. Providers bill per token, and the attention step (next tabs) compares every token with every other token, so doubling the length roughly quadruples that part of the work.

Meaning Map

From ID numbers to meaning

A token ID like 4389 is just a label. The number itself means nothing: token 4389 isn't "bigger" or "later" than token 12. So the model's first step is to look up each ID in a giant table and swap it for a list of a few thousand numbers called an embedding vector. Each row of the table belongs to one token, and the table is learned during training like every other part of the model.

Like coordinates on a city map

Think of a city map. Every shop has coordinates. Bakeries cluster in one neighborhood, auto shops line the highway, and bookstores sit near the university. If you know a shop's coordinates, you already know a lot about what kind of shop it is, just from where it sits and what's around it.

Embeddings are coordinates on a map of meaning, except the map has thousands of directions instead of two. Nobody draws this map by hand. Training starts every token at a random spot and nudges the coordinates, trillions of times, in whichever direction helps the model predict the next token a little better. Tokens that show up in similar sentences get nudged into the same neighborhood. "Easement" ends up near "adverse possession" because both appear in sentences about land, boundaries and title. No one ever labeled them "property law."

Measuring closeness

The standard closeness measure is cosine similarity: think of each vector as an arrow from the center of the map, and ask whether two arrows point the same way. Same direction scores 1, unrelated scores about 0, opposite directions score −1. Arrow length is ignored, so a token that appears constantly isn't automatically "close" to everything. Searching by meaning just means turning the query into a vector and finding the stored vectors whose arrows point most nearly the same way.

One word, several meanings

The lookup table gives "will" one starting vector, whether it means a testament or the future tense. The layers that follow (the Attention tab) adjust each token's vector based on its neighbors, so after a few layers "will" in "the testator's will" and "will" in "I will go" have moved to different neighborhoods. These adjusted vectors are called contextual embeddings, and they are why modern models handle ambiguity fairly well.

Two kinds of embeddings

Inside an LLM, every token gets its own vector. Search systems use a second kind: one vector for a whole passage, produced by a separate embedding model trained so that a question and the passage that answers it land close together. RAG uses that second kind. The toy below shows the idea with legal terms squashed down to two dimensions.

Attention Spotlight

The problem attention solves

After the lookup step every token has a vector, but each one was looked up alone. The vector for "it" is identical whether "it" means a contract, a dog or the weather. To understand a sentence, each word has to take in information from the words around it. Attention is the mechanism that does that mixing, and it is the core of the transformer, the design every modern LLM uses.

A party where everyone is looking for someone

Picture a room with one person for each word of the sentence: "The court held that the clause was void because it penalized a beneficiary." Everyone carries three things.

A sign held up in front of them saying what they're looking for. This is the query. "It" holds a sign reading: looking for a thing, a noun, probably something that can be void.

A name tag saying what they are. This is the key. "Clause" wears a tag reading: thing, document part. "Court" wears: thing, institution, does things. "Held" wears: action, past tense.

A backpack full of information about themselves. This is the value. When someone decides you're relevant, they copy some of what's in your backpack into their own.

Each person reads the name tag of everyone who came before them, compares it with their own sign, and gives it a match score. Then they copy from everyone's backpacks in proportion to those scores: mostly from the best matches, a little from the rest. After one round, "it" is no longer a blank pronoun. Its vector now holds a large dose of "clause" and a small dose of "court." Later words that need to know what "it" is can now find that out from "it" directly.

What "scoring for relevance" means, exactly

The match score is a dot product. Think of a shopping list and a store's shelves, both written over the same items. Your list says how much you want of each item: 2 loaves of bread, 1.5 pounds of cheese, no eggs, and you actively don't want fish (−0.5). The store's shelf count says how much of each item it stocks. Multiply want × stock for each item and add everything up. A store stocked with exactly what you want scores high. A store full of things you don't care about scores near zero. A store full of fish scores negative.

The query is the shopping list, each key is a store's shelves, and the "items" are the thousands of numbers in the vectors. That one multiply-and-add is the entire relevance test.

Turning scores into shares

Raw scores can be any size, including negative, so they get converted into shares that add up to 100% using softmax: raise the number e (about 2.718) to the power of each score, then divide each result by the total. Exponentiating makes every number positive and exaggerates the gaps, so a clear winner takes most of the share rather than an even split.

One correction happens first: every score is divided by the square root of the vector length. Long shopping lists naturally produce bigger totals, and without this the top score would grab nearly 100% every time, which makes training unstable.

Where the signs, tags and backpacks come from

Nobody writes rules like "pronouns look for nouns." Each word's vector is multiplied by three learned matrices to produce its query, key and value. A matrix here is a recipe for turning one list of numbers into another: each output number is a weighted mix of all the input numbers. Training adjusts those three recipes so that queries and keys that help predict the next word end up matching. Linking "it" to "clause" helps predict that the next words will be about enforceability rather than adjournment, so training strengthens that link.

Many heads, many layers

One round of matching at the party is one attention head. A model runs many heads side by side, often dozens, each with its own three recipes, so each learns to look for something different: one tracks pronouns, another subjects and verbs, another quotation marks or list numbering. Their results are combined into one updated vector per word.

That combined step plus a small per-word calculation (the feed-forward network, where much of the model's stored knowledge is thought to live) makes one layer. The whole thing repeats in the next layer, dozens of times. Each layer starts from vectors that already absorbed context in the layers before, so later layers can ask more abstract questions, like whether a sentence is a holding or dicta.

Looking only backward

While generating text, a token may only look at tokens that came before it. The model writes left to right, and during training it must not peek at the very word it's being asked to predict. A mask enforces this by setting every score for a future word to minus infinity, which softmax turns into exactly zero.

Next-Word Dice

The last step: from numbers back to words

After the final layer, the model takes the vector for the last token on the desk and multiplies it by one more big table, the unembedding matrix. Out comes one score for every token in the vocabulary, about 100,000 scores. These raw scores are called logits. Higher means "more likely to come next." Softmax, the same pie-slicing step used in attention, turns them into probabilities that add up to 1.

Rolling a loaded die

The model doesn't have to take the top token. Usually it samples. Imagine a die with 100,000 faces, each face sized by its probability: "dismiss" covers half the die, "compel" a sixth, "banana" a sliver too thin to see. Roll it, write down the winning token, add it to the text, then run the entire model again to build the die for the next token.

A 300-word answer takes roughly 400 rolls, and every roll needs a full pass through all the layers. That's why answers appear word by word. Real systems save time by caching each token's keys and values (the name tags and backpacks) so they aren't recomputed on every pass.

Temperature: reshaping the die

Temperature reshapes the die before the roll by dividing every logit by a number T. At T = 1 nothing changes. Below 1 the gaps between logits get bigger, so the favorite face grows and output becomes predictable; near 0 the model always takes the top choice, which is called greedy decoding. Above 1 the gaps shrink, the die gets fairer, and output becomes more surprising, including more surprisingly wrong.

Think of picking a restaurant. At low temperature you always go to your favorite. At high temperature you sometimes try the place with no reviews, which occasionally is wonderful and often isn't.

Top-p: trimming the die

Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, trims the die instead of reshaping it. Keep the most likely faces until their probabilities add up to, say, 90%, throw the rest away, and roll among what's left. That cuts off the long tail of absurd options without changing the order of the leaders.

How the die got loaded

The die's shape comes from training, in three stages. In pretraining the model reads trillions of tokens. At each position its predicted die is compared with the token that actually came next, and the error is measured as cross-entropy: roughly, how surprised the model was by the right answer. Gradient descent then nudges every weight slightly in the direction that would have made it less surprised. Think of learning to cook by guessing the next ingredient in millions of recipes and adjusting your instincts a hair every time you're wrong.

In instruction tuning the model trains further on examples of good questions and answers, so it responds like an assistant instead of simply continuing your document. In preference tuning, people or automated graders compare pairs of answers, and the model is pushed toward the preferred one. That last stage is where habits like admitting uncertainty get trained in, and also where a pull toward safe, conventional answers comes from (see the Limits tab).

Why hallucinations happen

Try the Case cite prompt. Think of an improv performer trained never to break the scene. Asked for a case name, the natural move is to produce something that sounds exactly like a case name, with the right reporter and a plausible year. The model was trained to produce text that looks like what usually comes next, and a well-formatted citation is what usually follows "the leading case is." Nothing in the sampling step checks whether that case exists. There is no lookup anywhere in the process, only probabilities. So the fixes have to come from outside the model: retrieval puts real sources on the desk (RAG), and verification tools like a citator check every citation before it's used (agents).

How the dice got loaded: training in three stages

1. Pretraining

Predict the next token across trillions of tokens of text. Self-supervised, very expensive. Produces a raw autocomplete engine that knows a lot.

2. Instruction tuning

Fine-tune on examples of questions with good answers so it behaves like an assistant instead of continuing your document.

3. Preference tuning

Humans (or rules) rank answers; reinforcement learning pushes toward the preferred ones. This is where "say you don't know" can get rewarded.

RAG Library

Closed book vs. open book

A plain LLM answers closed-book, from patterns absorbed in training. That knowledge stops at a cutoff date, has gaps, and can't be cited, because the model doesn't remember where it learned anything. Retrieval-augmented generation makes the exam open-book: find the relevant pages first, put them on the desk, and tell the model to answer from them.

Before any question: chunking and indexing

Your documents are cut into passages called chunks, usually a few paragraphs each, like cutting a treatise into index cards. Each chunk goes through an embedding model, which turns it into a vector, a point on the meaning map from the Embeddings tab. The vectors are stored in a vector index, a database built to find nearest neighbors quickly among millions of points.

Picture a library shelved by topic rather than alphabet, so everything about no-contest clauses sits on one shelf even when the titles share no words. Chunk size is a real tradeoff: small cards are precise but can split an argument in half; big cards keep context but blur the match and take up more desk space.

At question time: retrieve, augment, generate

Retrieve. Your question goes through the same embedding model and becomes a vector. The index returns the k chunks whose vectors point most nearly the same way. Many systems also run an old-fashioned keyword search in parallel and merge the two lists ("hybrid search"), because embeddings sometimes miss exact strings like a statute number.

Augment. The harness assembles a prompt: instructions ("answer only from the sources below, cite them, say so if they don't answer the question"), then the retrieved chunks with labels like [1] and [2], then your question. That bundle is everything the model sees. The toy shows it in the black box.

Generate. The model writes its answer token by token as always. With the passages on the desk, the most probable continuations are ones that restate and cite them. Citations work because each chunk carries a label and the model copies the label, the same copying behavior you can watch in the 3D Run.

What RAG can't fix

The answer is only as good as what was retrieved. If the right chunk isn't in the top k, the model never sees it. If the collection simply doesn't contain the answer, a well-built system says so and a poorly built one falls back on memory. Retrieval also sets the boundary of what gets considered at all: if the search is filtered to one jurisdiction, nothing from anywhere else reaches the desk (more on that in Limits). And the model can still misread or overstate a source, so citation checking still matters.

Agent Loop-de-Loop

From answering to doing

A chatbot takes one turn: you ask, it answers. An agent takes many turns on its own. It decides what to look up, looks it up, reads the result, decides again, and keeps going until it judges the job finished or hits a limit.

The chef and the runner

Picture a chef who isn't allowed to leave the kitchen. The chef thinks out loud about what the dish needs, writes an order on a slip ("two lemons, one bunch of thyme"), and passes it through the window. A runner reads the slip, goes to the market, and comes back with the groceries and a receipt. The chef looks at what arrived and decides the next step. The chef never touches the market, and the runner never cooks.

The chef is the LLM. The runner is the harness: ordinary software, written by people, wrapped around the model. The market is the set of tools: a search engine, a citator, a code runner, an email client.

What actually happens, one step at a time

1. The menu of tools. Before you type anything, the harness writes a system prompt that describes each tool in plain language plus a schema: the tool's exact name and the fields it accepts. For example, kb_search takes a query (text) and a top_k (a number).

2. The task. Your request is appended, and the harness sends the whole conversation to the model.

3. The model writes an order slip. The model generates tokens as usual. Sometimes it writes reasoning ("I should check the firm memos first"). When it wants a tool, it writes a structured block that follows the schema, usually JSON between special marker tokens. It learned that format during fine-tuning, the same way it learned the format of a business letter. At this point the model has not run anything. It has only written text that looks like a tool request.

4. The harness runs the tool. The harness watches the output stream. When it sees the closing marker, it stops the model, parses the JSON, checks it against the schema, and calls the real function with those arguments. This is everyday code, like an app querying a database.

5. The receipt comes back. Whatever the tool returns (search hits, an error message, a citator report) is converted to text and appended to the conversation as a tool result.

6. The model is called again, from the top. The model is stateless: it keeps nothing between calls. So the harness sends the entire, now-longer conversation again, and the model rereads all of it, including its own earlier reasoning and every result so far. Its memory of the task is nothing more than this growing transcript on the desk.

7. The loop stops when the model writes a final answer with no tool request, or when the harness hits a limit: a maximum number of steps, a full context window, or a rule that pauses for a human to approve something risky like filing or sending.

Does an agent create other AIs?

Sometimes an agent hands part of a job to a subagent, the way a cook might ask someone else to prep the vegetables. Mechanically, this is just one more tool call. The model writes something like delegate(task="find sister-state cases on X"). The harness responds by starting a brand-new conversation with the same model (or a smaller, cheaper one): a fresh system prompt, the delegated task as the first message, and its own tools. That conversation runs its own loop to completion, and its final answer is pasted back into the original conversation as a tool result.

No code is written to create a new model, and nothing new is trained. The weights are a fixed file sitting on a server, and every conversation, parent or subagent, is another run of that same file on different text. Think of one chef working several order tickets at once, with a separate notepad for each.

Why agents catch mistakes plain LLMs make

A tool result is information from outside the model. When a citator answers "NOT FOUND," that is a fact no amount of probability could have produced on its own. Step through the run below, then flip Skip the citator to see what slips through without that check.

One agent job, token by token

This tab runs a complete agent job, "build me a calculator in python," and lets you watch it one token at a time. Every other tab shows up here: tokens land on the desk, attention pulls information from earlier tokens, the model scores the vocabulary, one token is picked, and the agent loop runs tools between turns.

How to read the scene

The floor is a meaning map like the one on the Embeddings tab, with neighborhoods for English words, Python keywords, names, operators, punctuation and tool syntax. Every token already in the context sits on the floor at its spot, and tokens that appear many times pile into little towers.

Height is probability. At each step a glowing hill rises over the part of the map where the next token is likely to come from. A tall narrow spike means the model is nearly certain: after def add(a, the next token is almost surely b. A low, wide hill means many reasonable options, like the first word of a reply.

Pink beams rise from the tokens the model is attending to most and meet in the gold sphere, the working vector for this step. A thicker beam means a bigger share of attention.

What to watch for

At the first ⟨call⟩ token the model chooses to call a tool instead of answering in chat, the moment a chatbot becomes an agent. When the test runner reports a failure, those error tokens immediately get the strongest beams. Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom, and use Top view to flatten the hill into a 2-D heatmap.

Simulated numbers

The tokens follow a written script of what a coding agent would plausibly produce. The probabilities and attention weights come from simple rules built to behave like a real model: copying earlier tokens, recency, an attention sink, near-certain syntax. A real model's numbers would differ, and its attention is spread over dozens of heads and layers.

Limits

What shows up in practice

When AI research tools are tested on real legal research problems, one pattern stands out in their first drafts. Hand one a novel question, an issue of first impression with no controlling authority in the jurisdiction, and the draft tends to stay home. It argues from the jurisdiction's general standards and loosely analogous in-state cases, then treats the lack of authority as a dead end.

What it usually leaves out is what an experienced advocate reaches for in exactly that situation: persuasive authority from other states that have already faced the question, and a public policy argument for why the court should adopt the proposed rule. Neither move is exotic. Both are uncommon, and that turns out to be the whole problem.

The model is trained on what already exists

An LLM learns by predicting the next token in text that has already been written. Every probability it assigns is, at bottom, a summary of how often similar text followed similar context in the past. Most briefs on most issues cite binding in-state authority, because most issues have some. Citing another state as persuasive authority is a small slice of legal writing. Open public policy arguments are a smaller slice still, since courts tend to disfavor them when precedent exists. So when the model reaches the point in a draft where an argument begins, most of the probability is piled on the familiar argument types, and the rare ones get a sliver of the die.

Think of a GPS trained only on the routes people have already driven. It's superb at the common routes. It will almost never suggest the unpaved road nobody takes, even when that road is the shortcut. A novel legal issue is, nearly by definition, a road with few tire tracks.

Four forces pull the same way

Frequency. The die is loaded toward common argument types, because training text is mostly common argument types. The first toy below shows this.

Decoding settings. Drafting tools usually run at a low temperature for consistency, which makes the most common choice even more dominant. Raising the temperature doesn't aim at the good rare argument; it boosts every unlikely option at once, including nonsense. The second toy shows why.

Preference tuning. Graders reward answers that look careful and conventional. A draft that leans on an out-of-state case or an open policy appeal can look like overreach, so the model learns to avoid those moves unless asked.

Retrieval scope. Research tools often filter searches by the user's jurisdiction. If out-of-state cases never get retrieved, they never reach the desk, and the model can't cite what it never sees, except from memory, which is where invented citations come from.

The conclusion

An LLM is a mirror of what has already been written. It is strongest at the typical argument and weakest at the edges, and novel law lives at the edges. It can recombine existing ideas in new ways, which sometimes looks like creativity, but it is always pulled toward the nearest well-worn pattern. An AI first draft makes a good floor and a poor ceiling.

Pushing past the limit

You can't change the weights, but you can change what's on the desk and what you ask for. Ask for the uncommon moves by name. Widen retrieval to all jurisdictions and to secondary sources. Have the model list many kinds of arguments before it drafts any of them. Ask it to critique its own draft for what an inventive advocate would add. Then keep an attorney in the loop, because a genuinely new theory still has to come from someone who understands why the existing law doesn't fit. The mitigation lab at the bottom shows how each step changes the draft.

Toy 2 · Temperature won't fix it

If the draft is too conventional, why not turn up the temperature? Because temperature has no idea which unlikely options are good. It shrinks every gap between likely and unlikely at once. The model's vocabulary also holds thousands of irrelevant continuations (off-topic text, garbled citations, wrong-genre phrasing), each individually tiny. As T rises, the good rare arguments grow a little and that crowd of junk grows a lot.

Toy 3 · AI first draft vs. an advocate's draft

Hypothetical issue (fictional State of Westmore): Does a beneficiary trigger a trust's no-contest clause by filing a petition that asks the court only to construe an ambiguous term? No Westmore appellate court has decided the question. Click the colors to highlight each kind of argument.

AI first draft

Westmore courts strictly construe no-contest clauses and enforce them to carry out the settlor's intent. See Doe v. Doe Family Trust (Westmore Ct. App.) (fictional). The Westmore Trust Code does not define "contest." Because no Westmore appellate court has addressed whether a petition for construction is a contest, the outcome is uncertain, and a court could find the clause triggered. Counsel should weigh the risk of forfeiture before filing.

Advocate's draft

Westmore strictly construes no-contest clauses to carry out the settlor's intent. The Trust Code does not define "contest," and no Westmore court has decided whether a construction petition is one. This is a question of first impression, and the Court should answer it no. Courts in other states that have faced the question have held that a petition seeking only construction of an ambiguous term is not a contest, because it seeks to carry out the settlor's intent rather than defeat it. See In re Calder Trust (fictional); Matter of Ostrow (fictional). The Uniform Probate Code and the Restatement point the same direction, refusing to enforce penalty clauses against beneficiaries who act with probable cause. Sound policy supports the rule. A beneficiary who asks a court what an ambiguous provision means is helping to effectuate the settlor's intent. Penalizing that request forces beneficiaries to guess, inviting the very litigation the clause was meant to prevent.

NERD MODE · why the edges get squeezed
A perfectly trained model reproduces the frequencies in its training data, given the same context:pT=1(a | c) = f(a | c)
So its logits act like log-frequencies, and temperature becomes a power:za = log f(a | c) + const  ⟹  pT(a) = f(a)1/TΣb f(b)1/T
With T < 1 the power is above 1, so small shares shrink faster than big ones. Expected appearances in N drafts:E[count(a)] = N · pT(a)
Temperature rescales every log-odds ratio by the same factor. It can't single out the good rare option:log pT(a)pT(b) = za − zbT
A source can only be cited if it was retrieved onto the desk or recalled from the weights, and recall is where invented citations come from:P(cite x) ≤ P(x ∈ Dq) + P(x recalled)
Why prompting works: asking changes the context c, and the frequencies conditional on the new context are different:f(out-of-state | c ⊕ "cite other states") ≫ f(out-of-state | c)
Where
a, bkinds of argument (or any candidate continuations)
cthe context: everything on the desk
f(a | c)how often a followed contexts like c in the training text
Ttemperature; drafting tools often run well below 1
Dqthe passages retrieved for query q
Caveatreal models don't match training frequencies perfectly, and preference tuning reshapes them further. The direction of the effect is the point.
